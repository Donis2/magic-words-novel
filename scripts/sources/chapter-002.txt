The riders came over the grey hills before noon, and the sound of their arrival reached the village long before they did. It was not the thunder of a great army, only the steady drum of iron-shod hooves on the old road, but to the people of Grey Stone it was a sound out of a forgotten age. Old Haryn left his hives and stood at his gate. Kett the blacksmith set down his hammer and wiped his hands on his apron. Children stopped their games and ran to their mothers, and the mothers drew them close and said nothing, for fear has a way of moving faster than a horse ever could.

At the head of the column rode a young officer in the black and silver of the Empire's magic guard. His name was Lex Donis, and he looked at the small village with eyes the color of iron under a pale winter sky. He did not hurry, and he did not smile. Behind him came ten riders, cloaked and quiet, and every one of them kept one hand near his blade. They had ridden hard through the night, and the frost still lay white on their shoulders, but they sat straight in the saddle, for they were soldiers of the crown, and soldiers of the crown did not show their weariness.

Grey Stone had no wall and no gate, only a low ridge of piled stones and a single narrow bridge over a thin stream. Donis raised one hand, and the column came to a stop without a word. He looked across the village, at the low roofs and the thin threads of smoke, and he saw no single thing out of place, and yet he did not like what he felt. The air was wrong. It was too still, too cold, too heavy, as if the whole valley were holding its breath and waiting for something to fall.

He dismounted in the square and let his horse drink from a frozen trough. The people gathered in a half-circle at a careful distance, and no one spoke until the headman, a grey old man bowed by years, shuffled forward and bared his head. He asked, in a thin and shaking voice, what the crown wanted of their humble village.

"We seek a light," Donis said. His voice carried flat and clear across the square. "Last night a fire, or something like a fire, rose over the northern wood. It was seen for many leagues. I am ordered to find its source, and my orders do not allow me to return without an answer."

A murmur ran through the crowd. The headman wrung his hands and said that the village knew only what the watchmen had seen from the hill, a pale glow among the pines, brief as a falling star. Nothing more, he said. He had told the same to every traveler who asked. But Donis was not asking as a traveler, and the headman knew it, and his hands would not be still.

Then Kett the blacksmith spoke from the edge of the crowd, and his voice was loud and hard as the iron he worked. "The Herbalist's girl was in the wood last night," he said. "She goes there every evening, alone, to gather her herbs by the old road. Ask her what she saw, my lord. Ask the daughter of Elys."

Felis heard her own name carried through the square like a blow, and her heart turned over beneath her ribs. She had come out at the first ringing of hooves, with a thick scarf bound tight around her head and pulled low over her right eye, and she had stood at the back of the crowd hoping, like a fool, that no one would look for her. Now every face in the square turned toward her, and she felt the weight of a hundred eyes, and she wanted nothing in the world but to be small enough to disappear.

She did not run, because running would have been a confession greater than any word. She walked forward instead, and every step cost her more than the last. The pain behind her eye was a low ember now, but it flared as the crowd parted, as if the thing inside her could feel the eyes upon it and wanted out. She kept her hand loose at her side and her face turned slightly down, and she stopped where the headman had stood, a girl of sixteen in a grey wool dress, before an officer of the Empire.

Donis looked at her for a long moment, and she could not read what she saw in his iron eyes. He asked her name, and she answered him steadily. She was Felis, daughter of Elys. Her father lay ill, and so each evening she walked to the wood to cut herbs by the old road. She had seen the light, she said. It had come from deep among the trees, white and golden and cold, and it had gone as quickly as it came. She had seen no fire fall from the sky, and no goddess walk the earth. She had only seen a light, and she had been afraid.

"Then you will show me where you stood," Donis said.

It was not a question, and she knew it. The headman began to protest, to say that the wood was no place for a sick man's daughter to go with armed men, but Donis raised one hand and the old man fell silent at once. There was no cruelty in the gesture, only the calm of a man who expected to be obeyed and was not often disappointed.

Felis turned and led the way, and Donis walked a pace behind her with two of his riders, across the square and out along the dirt path where the village ended and the trees began. The crowd watched them go and said nothing. Even Kett the blacksmith, who had spoken her name so loudly a moment before, had no more words. There is a kind of silence, Felis thought, that is heavier than any stone, and it followed them all the way to the edge of the wood.

The path was changed now. She felt it the moment her feet left the trodden earth and found the pine needles, soft and deep and strange beneath her. The forest was watching her. She did not know how she knew this, but she knew it, the way a child knows it is being watched from the dark. The threads of magic that she had seen the night before had faded to nothing in the daylight, and yet she felt them still, humming just beyond the reach of her eyes, waiting.

She stopped where the old shrine stood, white and patient among the trees, and she pointed. "There. I stood there, and the light came from beyond the shrine, deep in the wood."

Donis walked past her to the shrine and laid one gloved hand on the cold stone of the Crystal Goddess. He stood very still for a long time, and his riders spread out behind him, their hands on their swords, their eyes on the trees. She saw him draw a slow breath, and she saw, for the first time, something flicker across his iron face. It was not fear. It was recognition, and it was old, and it frightened her more than fear ever could.

"So it has begun again," he said, so low that only she could hear. "The old magic is waking in the north."

He turned to look at her then, and in that moment the scarf slipped, no more than a finger's width, but it was enough. The last of the day's light caught her right eye, and the eye caught the light, and for the space of a single heartbeat the whole clearing was painted gold.

Donis went very still. The riders behind him went still too, and the air between them grew sharp as a drawn blade. She saw his hand move toward his sword, and she saw him stop it, and she saw the two things that warred across his face: the duty that had ridden with him out of the garrison gate, and something else, something older and less certain, that she could not name.

"Your eye," he said quietly. "When did it change?"

"Last night," she whispered. She did not lie. There was no lie left in her, and no strength to make one. "After the light. I do not know what it is, or what it means. I only know that I can see them now. The threads. The rivers of light in every living thing. I could not see them before, and now I cannot stop seeing them, and I am afraid."

For a long, terrible moment she was sure he would draw his sword, sure that her story would end here, at the foot of a silent goddess, with her secret laid bare in the snow. But Donis did not draw. He looked at her the way a man looks at a door that has suddenly opened onto a road he thought was closed forever, and he spoke in a voice that was not quite even.

"Then you must not let them take you," he said. "The Empire does not reward those who wake the old power without the crown's blessing. An eye like yours is worth more to the court than your father's life twice over, and the men who collect such things do not ask before they take."

He turned away and looked toward the north, where the white light had risen the night before, and where, far beyond the hills, a tomb older than the Empire lay waiting in the dark. "The light was not a curse, girl. And it was not a blessing. It was a door, and it has opened in you. Whether it leads to your life or your death is no longer for you to choose."

She did not understand him, not fully. But she understood enough. The old world she had known was already gone, carried off in the wind like smoke from the village fires, and the road ahead ran north, toward the Great Tomb, toward the Mad Hero and the Valkyrie and all the old fears men whispered over late fires. She looked at the soldiers, at their iron and their stillness, and at the young officer who had not yet made up his mind what she was.

"Then what will you do with me?" she asked.

Donis was silent for a long time. When at last he spoke, his voice was very low. "I will do what I was sent to do," he said. "Find the source of the light, and silence it. I have found the source."

He turned back to face her, and his iron eyes met her single golden one across the snow, and the whole world hung quiet between them for one heartbeat, and two.

"Now I must decide," he said, "whether to silence it. Or to ride with it north."
