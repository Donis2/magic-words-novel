Long before the Loranth Empire rose to rule the wide continent of Melolis, the Creator Orox shaped this world from stone and water, from wind and fire. In those first days, magic was not a rare and precious gift. It was a light, a warm fire that every soul carried within, and men and women walked the land without fear of the dark. The fields were rich, the rivers ran clean, and the gods watched kindly from their high places. But ages passed, blood was spilled, and that inner light grew dim. What was once common became legend, and legend, in time, became myth.

At the far edge of the Empire, where the paved roads became dirt trails and the great cities gave way to forest, stood a small village called Grey Stone. The old mountains watched over it with patient eyes, and a dark and ancient wood pressed close against its walls. Here lived Felis Elys, a girl of sixteen, whose hands could work but never worked magic. In a world where even a child could call a spark of fire from nothing, she alone was empty. Or so the whole village said, and she had learned to believe it.

Her father had fallen ill, and there was no one else to tend him. So each evening, when the light began to fail, Felis walked alone into the forest to gather the herbs that grew beside the old road. She was not brave, she told herself. She was only a daughter who feared the dark and walked into it anyway, because love is a kind of courage, and courage is stronger than fear. She cooked the thin stew over a low fire, ground the bitter herbs with a stone, and watched the shadows gather in the corners of the small room, and she did these quiet things without complaint, for they were all she had.

The forest was ancient and full of shadows. That night, as she stepped beneath the tall trees, the wind fell completely still. No bird sang. No leaf stirred. The path she had walked a hundred times felt strange and wrong, as if the earth itself were holding its breath. She tightened her hand on the small iron knife at her belt and went on, though every step cost her something.

Deep in the wood stood a shrine to the Crystal Goddess, a figure of white stone whose blind eyes stared along the road. The people of Grey Stone left small gifts there, a crust of bread, a few silver coins, a handful of grain, and prayed to the goddess for a good harvest and a mild winter. Felis knelt on the soft moss and laid her own poor offering at the statue's feet: a bundle of herbs and one worn copper coin.

It was then that she saw it. A thread of gold, thin as a strand of hair, drifting through the dark air like a living thing. It glowed, and it moved against the wind. She blinked and rubbed her eyes, sure that fear and hunger had tricked her. But the thread did not vanish. She turned her head, and the whole forest opened before her like a great book: rivers of light, blue and gold and green, flowing through every leaf, every root, and every stone. She could see magic.

A sharp pain burned behind her right eye, sudden and bright as a flame. She pressed both hands to her face and cried out, and the pain roared through her skull, and the earth trembled beneath her knees. The rivers of light surged toward her, poured into her, and the world went white as snow.

When she opened her eyes again, the pain was gone, and the wood was quiet once more. But something in her had changed. Her right eye was no longer its ordinary grey. The iris shone a pale and burning gold, ringed with a faint light. And where the moon fell, she could still trace the thin threads of magic moving in the air, soft as breath over water. She was empty no longer. Something had awakened inside her, and she did not know whether it was a gift or a curse.

Near dawn she reached home and found her father sleeping. She stood beside his bed for a long time and said nothing. In the grey light, her golden eye caught its own reflection in the window, and she turned away. She could not tell him. Not yet. How could she explain what she did not understand herself?

She slept little. All through that pale morning she sat by the cold hearth, testing her wrong eye. When she looked at an old pot, she saw the faint lines of heat around it, the memory of fire still woven into the iron. When she looked at her own hands, she saw a slow river of light running beneath her skin, thin and blue and patient. It frightened her. But under the fear was something else, something bright and new, and she could not bring herself to look away.

Her father woke near noon and asked in a weak voice why she had let the fire go out. She only shook her head and went to fetch water from the well. In the bright square she passed a group of farmers, and one of them, a blacksmith named Kett, stopped his work and stared at her as she went by. His hammer hung still in the air. Felis kept her face down and walked faster, but she felt his eyes upon her back the whole way, and a cold fear crept up her spine.

For the news had already begun to run. By noon the whole village had heard of the light over the northern wood. Some said it was an omen. Some said the goddess had walked the earth. Old Haryn, who kept bees at the edge of the trees, swore he had seen a fire fall from the sky and disappear among the pines. Fear spread faster than truth, and truth, as always, arrived late. And into that fear Felis walked, small and quiet, carrying her secret like a coal held close to the chest.

Near the well, Kett the blacksmith stepped into her path and folded his thick arms. "You look pale, girl," he said slowly. "And there is something wrong about your face." Felis gripped the rope of the bucket and said nothing. For a long moment she feared he would reach out and lift her chin, and then all the village would see the gold. But a dog barked, a cart rumbled past, and the moment broke. Kett shook his head and let her go, and whispered to the men beside him as she hurried away, her heart beating hard and fast against her ribs.

Far away, at the iron gates of the nearest garrison town, a watchman had seen the sky flicker over the northern forest, bright as a second dawn. Word traveled faster than reason. By sunrise the report had reached the officer's hall, and riders in the black and silver of the Empire's magic guard thundered out of the gate, led by a young officer the soldiers called Donis. He did not smile. His eyes were the color of iron, and his face was hard as stone.

They said the last tomb of Tartalos had stirred in the deep north, and that the dead did not sleep so easily since the long war of the Southern Gods. They spoke in low voices of the Mad Hero, of the Valkyrie, of omens and old magic and the end of a quiet age. Lex Donis cared little for such legends. He had one order, and one order only: find the source of the strange light, and silence it before word reached the Emperor in his far palace. In the Empire, a magic eye that woke without the blessing of the crown was a danger, not a wonder, and it was his duty to put such dangers down.

He did not yet know that the source of that light was a girl of sixteen with a golden eye and a father who could not rise from his bed. He did not know that their two lives were about to be bound together, and that the meeting would set a great and terrible journey in motion, a quest that would carry them from the green edge of the Empire to the black mouth of the Great Tomb itself.

As morning climbed over the grey hills, Felis stood at the edge of the village with ash on her hands and wonder in her chest, and she watched the riders come. There would be no more quiet evenings, no more simple walks to the shrine. Her old life had ended at the foot of a stone goddess. A new one was beginning now, born of fire and light and a power she could not yet name. And she was afraid. And she was not afraid.
